Overview

Labs sequence the DNA of bacteria and fungi found in patients, and Solu analyses it. In minutes, it tells labs what the infection is, which antibiotics will fail, and whether patients are infecting each other.

The challenge

Customers were moving from a handful of samples to thousands, and from one lab to multi-hospital programmes.

My role

First designer. I set up product design processes, designed end-to-end features and worked on the product vision.

02 Spread Jul — Sep 2026

Is resistance jumping between patients?

Outbreak tools compare bacteria like family trees. But bacteria also swap plasmids: small loops of DNA that work like USB sticks and can carry resistance between unrelated bacteria. Two patients can share the same dangerous plasmid while their bacteria look unrelated.

Evidence firstFive customers had asked for it unprompted, including a national CDC. No deal depended on it, so we built it as depth inside the existing product and parked a bigger, riskier market.
In research toolsA figure that’s hard to readHow plasmids are usually shown: dozens of labels, no hierarchy, and nothing that says where the resistance is. Below is how we designed it at Solu.

03 Information architecture Sep 2026 → 2027

How do we work with thousands of samples?

I led an information architecture project: an audit of how Solu was organised, a model of what it’s made of, and three directions for the future. Every feature had become another page or tab, and the navigation mirrored how data was stored. Fine with 26 samples, unworkable with thousands.

The insightEverything in Solu is a sample or a group of samples. What was missing was a way to filter them, “resistant samples from this ward, last month”, and save that as a view that keeps itself up to date.
Three directions, lo-fi Scroll · open full screen to see every screen
A · MonitorA home that shows the state of everything. Closest to today, but passive if nobody looks.
B · SliceFilter samples and save them as live views. Most flexible, with some setup effort.
C · InvestigateOpen a case for an outbreak, assisted by AI. Most powerful, and the heaviest to build.
DecisionI recommended combining all three into one system, built in the order labs need them: Slice, then Monitor, then Investigate. Slicing comes first, because the monitoring home and the investigation cases both run on saved views. AI comes last: highest impact, highest cost, and it needs saved views as examples to learn from.

04 Expand Oct 2026 → now

Can a whole country’s labs work in one place?

Learn the domain in publicI asked our cofounder to teach the whole team the biology before we designed plasmids. The whole team got sharper at the problem.
Evidence over enthusiasmChecking that no deal depended on plasmids kept us from building a second product we couldn’t support.
What I’d do differentlyOn quality, I questioned pass/fail too late and let scope grow. I now design the unhappy paths first and split releases early.
ReflectionI loved learning a field as complex as microbiology. I believe that hospitals should spend their time saving lives, not decoding badly designed tools, and that made this work deeply meaningful to me.
